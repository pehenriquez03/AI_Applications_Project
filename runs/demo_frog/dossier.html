Deprioritize. The paper (Frog in the Pan, Da, Gurun and Warachka, 2014) argues that momentum is stronger when a stock's past return built up through many small daily moves than through a few large jumps, and we tested a long-only version on a 415-name S&P 500 survivor universe over 2015-2025. The strategy earned a net CAGR of 11.87% against 12.71% for the equal-weight universe, with a Sharpe ratio of 0.58 against 0.65, and the edge over the equal-weight universe fails at 10 bps and holds in 0 of 2 sub-periods. The simulated fund economics are uncertain: P(NPV > 0) is 43% and the median incremental fee PV is -$496k versus the equal-weight universe fund. The recommendation rests mainly on negative historical performance and weak robustness, with an amber implementation reliability because 4 fields were assumed and 6 adapted.

Narrative model-written

What the paper proposes

The paper's frog-in-the-pan hypothesis is that investors are inattentive to information that arrives continuously in small amounts and process it with a delay. Momentum should therefore be stronger and more persistent after continuous information than after discrete information, for stocks with similar cumulative formation-period returns. The selected experiment is Table 2, Panel A: a sequential double sort on past return (PRET) quintiles, then information discreteness (ID) quintiles, with six-month winners-minus-losers returns per ID quintile. ID is the sign of PRET times the share of negative days minus the share of positive days, so low ID means continuous information. The paper reports momentum of 5.94% in the low ID quintile versus –2.07% in the high ID quintile, an 8.01% difference with a t-statistic of 8.54, on CRSP data in the post-1927 sample.

What we implemented and changed

We built the signal from PRET and ID over a 12 month formation window with a 1 month skip, held for 6 months with overlapping monthly cohorts, and bought the highest-PRET names in the lowest-ID group. The test differs from the paper in several ways. We used equal weighting where the paper does not state a weighting. We used the long-only favored group with no short leg. We sorted past return into quintiles and then used terciles on ID with a 15-name floor, because quintile-within-quintile leaves too few names in a 415-name universe. Breakpoints are equal-count over eligible names, since our universe has no exchange or size breakpoints. The universe is today's S&P 500 constituents (415 names after 19 were excluded for missing data), eligible from their index add date, over 2015-01-01 to 2025-12-31. Four fields were assumed (the formation window, no price floor, a minimum history of 252 days, and 15 formation days) and 6 were adapted. The paper's $5 price filter was not applied. Nine details the paper leaves open were left to engine defaults, including weighting, overlap handling, breakpoint universe, share-code filters, the treatment of PRET equal to zero, and delisting returns.

What the backtest found

Over 132 months of portfolio formation, the strategy earned a net CAGR of 11.87% against 12.71% for the equal-weight universe, with a Sharpe ratio of 0.58 against 0.65. The strategy's net maximum drawdown was -0.350831. The four-factor alpha t-statistic is -0.99, and the edge over the equal-weight universe fails at 10 bps. It holds in 0 of 2 sub-periods. Across the three ID groups the ranked monotonicity check has a group rho of 1.00 in the expected direction, which means the group ordering matches the paper's sign, but the favored group is the one that is held and it still trails the benchmarks after costs. Mean monthly turnover was 0.139816.

What the simulation and economics found

The Monte Carlo resamples historical days in stationary blocks over 1000 paths and applies an assumed fee schedule with a hard hurdle to a long-only fund. That schedule is an assumption, not an observed fee schedule, and incremental fees are measured against an equal-weight universe fund, never against SPY. P(NPV > 0) is 43%, and the median incremental fee PV is -$496k versus the equal-weight universe fund. Paths beat SPY in a fraction of 0.029 and beat the equal-weight fund in a fraction of 0.418. Expected incremental fees are not positive, so no break-even AUM is reported. The simulation cannot create regimes absent from the sample, and the sample lies entirely after publication.

Evidence checks passed for 100% of the 16 material fields checked, with a mean field agreement of 1.0 and 0 critical check disagreements, so the extraction of the paper is internally consistent. Other limitations are the survivor universe, which flatters returns because delisted names are absent, equal weighting, the long-only adaptation, the post-publication sample, zero yield on cash, and Yahoo adjusted-close return conventions.

What to do next

Do not allocate further research time to this strategy as specified. The test found no net edge over the equal-weight universe, and the fund economics do not favor it. If the idea is revisited, the more informative test would use a point-in-time universe that includes delisted names, and the short leg and the paper's full quintile-by-quintile sort on a broader stock universe, since the long-only, terciles-on-ID version on 415 survivors cannot confirm or refute the paper's result.
