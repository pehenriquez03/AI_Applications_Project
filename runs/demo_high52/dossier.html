Deprioritize. The strategy is the 52-week high momentum rule from George and Hwang (2004): rank stocks by the ratio of current price to the highest price of the past 12 months and hold the top 30% for 6 months. Our long-only test on a 415-name survivor universe over 2015 to 2025 returned a net CAGR of 10.57% against 12.71% for the equal-weight universe, with a Sharpe ratio of 0.57 against 0.65. The economics are also negative: the probability that the fund's incremental fees beat the equal-weight fund after research cost is 15%, with a median incremental fee PV of -$2.1M. The main reason for the recommendation is that the strategy fails to beat a passive equal-weight basket of the same names, and the edge does not hold in either sub-period.

Narrative model-written

What the paper proposes

George and Hwang (2004) argue that traders anchor on the 52-week high when judging news. Near the high they are slow to bid prices up as far as good news warrants, and far from it they are slow to sell as low as bad news implies, so the information is priced in gradually and returns continue in the same direction. The paper ranks all CRSP stocks on the ratio of the price at the end of month t-1 to the highest price in the 12 months ending then. The selected experiment is the (6, 6) strategy in Table I: an equally weighted portfolio long the top 30% and short the bottom 30%, held 6 months with overlapping monthly cohorts and no skip month. The paper reports winner 1.51%, loser 1.06% and winner minus loser 0.45% per month (t = 2.00) for 1963 to 2001.

What we implemented and changed

We kept the signal, the 252-day window, the top 30% selection, equal weighting, the 6-month holding period, monthly rebalancing and the zero skip months. We changed four things. The universe is 415 current S&P 500 names, with 19 excluded for missing data, and each name is eligible only from its index add date, so it is a survivor universe. The sample runs from 2015-01-01 to 2025-12-31 rather than the paper's period. The strategy is long-only: we hold the favored group and do not short the losers, so the test is not the paper's winner-minus-loser spread. Breakpoints are equal-count sorts of eligible names rather than the paper's CRSP-wide cutoffs. We also assumed three defaults the paper does not state: no price floor, a minimum of 252 days of history, and a minimum of 15 trading days in a formation month. Seven details remain unresolved in the paper, including whether the 12-month high uses closing or intraday prices, how prices are adjusted for splits and distributions, and how delistings are treated. We used the engine's default conventions for these and use Yahoo adjusted-close returns. Costs are 10 bps, and cash earns zero yield.

What the backtest found

Net of costs, the strategy earned a CAGR of 10.57% against 12.71% for the equal-weight universe, and its Sharpe ratio was 0.57 against 0.65. The relative monthly return difference versus the equal-weight universe is not statistically distinguishable from zero over the 132 months, but its sign is negative. The four-factor alpha t-statistic is -1.17, so there is no evidence of alpha after market, size, value and momentum exposure. The edge over the equal-weight universe fails at 10 bps and holds in 0 of 2 sub-periods. The group monotonicity check goes the wrong way: the group rho is -1.00, not in the expected direction, and the group with the lowest price-to-high ratio had a higher mean monthly return than the group with the highest ratio. That is the opposite of the paper's finding. The sample lies entirely after publication, which may explain some decay of the effect. The survivor universe flatters returns, so the true result is likely no better than this.

What the simulation and economics found

We ran 1000 bootstrap paths with stationary blocks of mean length 126 days over a 5-year horizon. The fund assumption is 2/20 with a 5% hard hurdle and a high-water mark, which is an assumption for a long-only fund and not an observed fee schedule. Incremental fees are measured against an equal-weight universe fund charged the same fees, never against SPY. On that basis P(NPV > 0) is 15%, and the median incremental fee PV is -$2.1M. Expected incremental fees are negative, so no break-even AUM exists. When we remove the historical shortfall versus the equal-weight fund, the share of positive-NPV paths rises to about half, which shows that the negative result comes from the measured underperformance and not from simulation noise alone. The bootstrap resamples historical days and cannot create regimes absent from the sample.
