Narrative template

What the paper proposes

The paper (Maxing Out: Stocks as Lotteries and the Cross-Section of Expected Returns) proposes MAX: the maximum daily return of the stock within the month (the portfolio formation month) as the signal, sorted by deciles, favoring the lowest MAX decile (portfolio 1, low MAX). Rationale: Investors have a preference for lottery-like payoffs (and many are poorly diversified), so they are willing to pay more for stocks that exhibit extreme positive daily returns, and these stocks therefore earn lower returns in the future.

What we implemented and changed

We adapted 5 fields and assumed 4. Adapted: required_inputs (market capitalization used only for weighting, breakpoints or filters); weighting (policy: equal weight everywhere); disfavored_group (policy: long-only, no short leg); universe_and_filters (policy: our 415-name S&P 500 survivor universe); sample_period (policy: our snapshot sample). Assumed: breakpoints set to equal-count over eligible names; price_floor set to none; min_history_days set to 252; min_formation_days set to 15. Portfolios were formed in 132 months with 37.8 names on average.

What the backtest found

Over the sample the strategy returned 6.31% a year net of costs, against 12.71% for the equal-weight universe and 13.45% for SPY. Its Sharpe ratio was 0.35 against 0.65 for the equal-weight universe, and its maximum drawdown was -34.1%. The alpha t-statistic was -1.60; it is reported, not used as a gate. Robustness across costs, sub-periods and ranked groups gave a rho of 0.84.

What the simulation and economics found

Across the simulated paths, incremental fee income versus the equal-weight universe fund had a median present value of -$5.0M, with a 5th to 95th percentile range of -$13.4M to -$425k. The share of paths with positive NPV after research cost was 2.5%, and 54.9% once the historical excess return is removed. Break-even AUM is not defined; it is reported, not headlined.
